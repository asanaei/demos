Multilevel modeling

Life satisfaction for simulated respondents, built from the World Values Survey. In four countries with 250 people each, one regression for everyone gets the sign of age wrong, and the slope of freedom of choice differs from country to country. In 65 countries with 30 people each, where the simulation makes the slopes differ more, random intercepts and slopes fit best on AIC and BIC.

The R script that simulated the data

The respondents are simulated. The script under the R code draws age, gender, freedom of choice, and education from summaries of each country's sample in the seventh wave of the World Values Survey (Haerpfer et al. 2024). Life satisfaction comes from a latent score whose thresholds reproduce each country's real shares of answers 1 to 10, with slopes calibrated to the real ones, except that age has the same slope in every country, as the real data allow. The 65-country example applies the same method to every country and territory in the wave except Iraq, whose file copies the answers on financial satisfaction into the income question. It draws 30 respondents from each, spreads the slopes of freedom 1.5 times as far from their average as in the real data, and pulls each country's level halfway toward the average level. With the real values, random intercepts and slopes did not come out best on both AIC and BIC in any of 72 simulated samples of 8 to 60 respondents per country: the real slopes differ little next to the real levels, so by the time BIC detects the slope differences, AIC prefers fixed effects. No real respondent appears in the data.
