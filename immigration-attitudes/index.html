Ten questions, one attitude?

The World Values Survey, an international academic survey, asks the same ten questions about the effects of immigration in dozens of countries. Pick the United States, surveyed in 2017, or Great Britain, surveyed in 2022, combine the answers into one index, and see what alpha and factor analysis say about whether one number can stand for them.

What to try

The numbers below are for the United States, the country shown first.

  • Turn off the reversal. Five questions run the other way: agreeing that immigrants increase crime is the less favorable answer. Left as they are, these questions pull against the rest, and alpha falls from 0.82 to 0.35. The first principal component and the one-factor scores do not change, because they work out the direction of each question from the data.
  • Compare one factor with two. An alpha of 0.82 is high enough for most readers to call the ten questions one scale. Yet a model with one common factor fits poorly: its RMSEA, a measure of misfit, is 0.144, where values above 0.10 are usually called poor. Two factors fit well (0.038), and parallel analysis also keeps two. One factor gathers the four statements of harm, the other the four statements of benefit.
  • Drop the asylum question. It goes least with the rest (item-rest correlation 0.20), and alpha rises slightly without it, from 0.82 to 0.83. Whether it belongs in an index of views on immigration is still a question about content, not about alpha.
  • Rotate. Try varimax, promax, oblimin, and geomin. The fit and the share of each question that the factors explain stay the same; only the loadings change. In the loading plot the questions keep their places relative to one another; only the lines of the factors move. Varimax keeps the axes at right angles; oblimin lets them lean toward each other until they meet at 62 degrees, and the cosine of that angle, 0.47, is the correlation between the two factors. All four rotations find the same two factors.
  • Ask for three factors. The third factor is a single question, the statement that immigration gives asylum to political refugees (Q125). The model explains 99.5% of the variation in its answers, the ceiling of the fitting procedure. Survey answers always contain some error, so a factor that explains one question completely is a sign of one factor too many.
  • See who holds which view. With the reversal on, a high score on either factor means a favorable view: seeing little harm, or seeing many benefits. Each additional level of education goes with a score 0.22 standard deviations higher on the benefit factor and 0.11 higher on the harm factor; a bachelor's degree is three levels above a high-school diploma. The average of all ten answers lands in between, at 0.19, and hides the difference. Age works the other way round: each decade goes with seeing more harm (0.09 standard deviations) and slightly fewer benefits (0.03). Below the regressions, a model that allows for a habit of agreeing tells a different story.
  • Open Advanced. With omega instead of alpha, the reliability of the index depends on the factor model: 0.82 with one factor, 0.85 with two. With polychoric correlations the loadings rise, for Q122 from 0.72 to 0.81; the benefit factor now comes first, but the regressions on the two factors barely move.
  • Switch to Great Britain. The ten questions split the same way, but parallel analysis and BIC both favor three factors there. The third is not a single question: it gathers the statements that immigration gives asylum to political refugees and helps poor people establish new lives, a humanitarian side of the benefits. In the model with a habit of agreeing, the habit shows no clear link with education in Britain, while in the United States it rises with it.

Data

The data come from the seventh wave of the World Values Survey, which interviewed people in 64 countries and territories between 2017 and 2022 (Haerpfer et al. 2022). The page uses two of its national surveys: the United States in 2017, with 2,596 respondents, and Great Britain in 2022, with 2,609; Northern Ireland was surveyed separately. In each, the page uses the respondents who answered all ten questions below, 2,455 in the United States and 2,407 in Britain, counting "don't know", refusals, and other missing codes as missing. The survey weights are not applied, so the numbers describe these respondents rather than all adults in either country.

Q121 Impact on the country
"Now we would like to know your opinion about the people from other countries who come to live in this country - the immigrants. How would you evaluate the impact of these people on the development of this country?" Five answers, coded from 1, the most negative, through 3, neither good nor bad, to 5, very good.
Q122 to Q129 Effects of immigration
"From your point of view, what have been the effects of immigrants on the development of this country?" Eight statements, each answered 0 = disagree, 1 = hard to say, 2 = agree. Four name a benefit: fill useful jobs in the workforce (Q122), strengthen cultural diversity (Q123), give asylum to political refugees (Q125), and help poor people establish new lives (Q127). Four name a harm: increase the crime rate (Q124), increase the risks of terrorism (Q126), increase unemployment (Q128), and lead to social conflict (Q129).
Q130 Who may come to work
"How about people from other countries coming here to work. Which one of the following do you think the government should do?" 1 = let anyone come who wants to, 2 = let people come as long as there are jobs available, 3 = place strict limits on the number of foreigners who can come here, 4 = prohibit people coming here from other countries.
Background
Education is the highest level completed, on the international ISCED scale from 0, none or early childhood education only, to 8, a doctorate; 3 is upper secondary school and 6 a bachelor's degree (Q275). Age is in years and enters per decade (Q262). Female is 1 for women (Q260). Born abroad is 1 for respondents who say they are immigrants to the country (Q263). The regressions use those who also answered these four questions: 2,402 in the United States, 251 of them born abroad, and 2,130 in Britain, 282 of them born abroad.

The survey's conditions of use do not allow its data files to be passed on, so the page holds no individual answers. It holds summary numbers computed from the file for each country: how many people gave each answer, the means and standard deviations, the correlations among the ten questions, their covariances with the four background questions, and the estimates of the model with a habit of agreeing. The R script that computed them is under the R code.

How the page computes

Everything on the page except the model with a habit of agreeing is computed in the browser from those summary numbers, with the formulas of the R packages psych and GPArotation; that model is estimated in R with the package lavaan. The R code below runs the same analysis on the survey file and gives the same numbers to three decimals, with two exceptions. Parallel analysis compares the data with random data, so its yardstick varies a little from run to run, and in close cases R and the page can keep different numbers of factors. With three or four factors, when one question ends up almost fully explained, R and the page can settle on different solutions that fit about equally well.

Notes on method

Alpha measures how consistently the questions in an index go together, from 0 to 1. It rises with the number of questions, and it can be high even when the questions measure two different things, as here. When the errors in the answers are unrelated, alpha is a lower bound on the reliability of the index, equal to it only if every question measures the same thing equally well. When the errors are related, alpha can be off in either direction: a habit of agreeing inflates it when all statements run one way, and deflates it when, as here, they run both ways and some are reversed. Omega, under Advanced, uses the loadings of a factor model and gives the share of the index's variation that the factors account for; it is only as good as that model.

A factor model looks for a few unobserved variables, the factors, that would explain the correlations among the questions. A loading says how strongly a question goes with a factor. With one factor, or with axes kept at right angles, it is the correlation between the two: near 0, the question has little to do with the factor; near 1, it follows the factor closely. With axes that lean it is the weight of the factor in the question, which is not a correlation. The page fits the model as psych does by default, by making the correlations the model predicts as close as possible to the observed ones ("minres"). RMSEA, TLI, and BIC compare how well models with different numbers of factors fit. Parallel analysis keeps each factor whose eigenvalue, a measure of how much of the shared variation the factor takes, is larger than in 95% of random data sets of the same size. Fitted to random data, a one-factor model often lets a single question take nearly the whole factor, so the yardstick for the first factor is close to 1; psych does the same. Chapter 6 of Revelle's online book is a readable guide to all of this, with R code (Revelle, in preparation).

Rotation changes the axes, not the model: every rotation of the same solution fits the data equally well. Varimax keeps the axes at right angles and makes the loadings on each factor as unequal as possible, a few large and the rest near zero. Promax, oblimin, and geomin let the axes lean toward each other, so that the factors can correlate. Promax raises the varimax loadings to the fourth power to make a target and then finds the leaning axes that come closest to it. Oblimin, the default in psych, and geomin, the default of the efa() function in the R package lavaan, each reward loadings near zero in their own way. Factor scores give each respondent a value on each factor. For two or more factors the page uses ten Berge scores, the default of psych's factor.scores() function, which keep the scores correlated as the factors are; with one factor it uses regression scores, which are proportional to them.

A correlation between questions with three answers understates how strongly the opinions behind them go together. Polychoric correlations, under Advanced, estimate the latter by treating each answer as a cut through an opinion that varies smoothly. The loadings rise, but which question goes with which hardly changes.

The two factors follow the direction of the statements: the statements of harm go together, and so do the statements of benefit. Part of this split can come from a habit of agreeing with whatever statement is put to the respondent. A common way to model such a habit, when a set of agree-disagree statements runs both ways, is to add a factor that raises agreement with every statement by the same amount and is unrelated to the views themselves; the page reports that model under the regressions, and its code is at the end of the R code. Its results depend on those assumptions, and here they are fragile: fitted to the answers as ordered categories, the same model gives impossible estimates in both countries. Holding both views is also possible: a respondent can believe that immigrants fill useful jobs and that they raise unemployment. The regressions describe who holds which view; they do not say why.
