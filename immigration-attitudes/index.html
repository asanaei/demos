Ten questions, one attitude?

In 2017 the World Values Survey, an international academic survey, asked adults in the United States ten questions about the effects of immigration. Combine the answers of the 2,455 who answered all ten into one index, then see what alpha and factor analysis say about whether one number can stand for them.

What to try
  • Turn off the reversal. Five questions run the other way: agreeing that immigrants increase crime is the less favorable answer. Left as they are, these questions pull against the rest, and alpha falls from 0.82 to 0.35. The first principal component and the one-factor scores do not change, because they work out the direction of each question from the data.
  • Compare one factor with two. An alpha of 0.82 is high enough for most readers to call the ten questions one scale. Yet a model with one common factor fits poorly: its RMSEA, a measure of misfit, is 0.144, where values above 0.10 are usually called poor. Two factors fit well (0.038), and parallel analysis also keeps two. One factor gathers the four statements of harm, the other the four statements of benefit.
  • Drop the asylum question. It goes least with the rest (item-rest correlation 0.20), and alpha rises slightly without it, from 0.82 to 0.83. Whether it belongs in an index of views on immigration is still a question about content, not about alpha.
  • Rotate. Try varimax, promax, oblimin, and geomin. The fit and the share of each question that the factors explain stay the same; only the loadings change. In the loading plot the questions stay put and the axes move. Varimax keeps the axes at right angles; oblimin lets them lean toward each other until they meet at 62 degrees, and the cosine of that angle, 0.47, is the correlation between the two factors. All four rotations find the same two factors.
  • Ask for three factors. The third factor is a single question, the statement that immigration gives asylum to political refugees (Q125). The model explains 99.5% of the variation in its answers, the ceiling of the fitting procedure. Survey answers always contain some error, so a factor that explains one question completely is a sign of one factor too many.
  • See who holds which view. With the reversal on, a high score on either factor means a favorable view: seeing little harm, or seeing many benefits. Each additional level of education goes with a score 0.22 standard deviations higher on the benefit factor and 0.11 higher on the harm factor; a bachelor's degree is three levels above a high-school diploma. The average of all ten answers lands in between, at 0.19, and hides the difference. Age works the other way round: each decade goes with seeing more harm (0.09 standard deviations) and slightly fewer benefits (0.03). The page offers two readings of the education gap below the regressions.
  • Open Advanced. With omega instead of alpha, the reliability of the index depends on the factor model: 0.82 with one factor, 0.85 with two. With polychoric correlations the loadings rise, for Q122 from 0.72 to 0.81, but the regressions on the two factors barely move.

Data

The data come from the seventh wave of the World Values Survey, which interviewed people in 64 countries and territories between 2017 and 2022 (Haerpfer et al. 2022). The survey in the United States, in 2017, had 2,596 respondents. The page uses the 2,455 who answered all ten questions below, counting "don't know" and refusals as missing. The survey's weights are not applied, so the numbers describe these respondents rather than all American adults.

Q121 Impact on the country
"Now we would like to know your opinion about the people from other countries who come to live in this country - the immigrants. How would you evaluate the impact of these people on the development of this country?" Five answers, coded from 1, the most negative, through 3, neither good nor bad, to 5, very good.
Q122 to Q129 Effects of immigration
"From your point of view, what have been the effects of immigrants on the development of this country?" Eight statements, each answered 0 = disagree, 1 = hard to say, 2 = agree. Four name a benefit: fill useful jobs in the workforce (Q122), strengthen cultural diversity (Q123), give asylum to political refugees (Q125), and help poor people establish new lives (Q127). Four name a harm: increase the crime rate (Q124), increase the risks of terrorism (Q126), increase unemployment (Q128), and lead to social conflict (Q129).
Q130 Who may come to work
"How about people from other countries coming here to work. Which one of the following do you think the government should do?" 1 = let anyone come who wants to, 2 = let people come as long as there are jobs available, 3 = place strict limits on the number of foreigners who can come here, 4 = prohibit people coming here from other countries.
Background
Education is the highest level completed, on the international ISCED scale from 0, none or early childhood education only, to 8, a doctorate; 3 is upper secondary school, in the United States a high-school diploma, and 6 a bachelor's degree (Q275). Age is in years and enters per decade (Q262). Female is 1 for women (Q260). Born abroad is 1 for respondents who say they are immigrants to the country (Q263). Of the 2,455, 2,402 answered all four and enter the regressions; 251 of them were born abroad.

The survey's conditions of use do not allow its data files to be passed on, so the page holds no individual answers. It holds summary numbers computed from the file: how many people gave each answer, the means and standard deviations, the correlations among the ten questions, and their covariances with the four background questions. The R script that computed them is under the R code.

How the page computes

Everything on the page is computed in the browser from those summary numbers, with the formulas of the R packages psych and GPArotation. The R code below runs the same analysis on the survey file and gives the same numbers to three decimals, with two exceptions. Parallel analysis compares the data with random data, so its yardstick varies a little from run to run, and in close cases R and the page can keep different numbers of factors. With three or four factors, when one question ends up almost fully explained, R and the page can settle on different solutions that fit about equally well.

Notes on method

Alpha measures how consistently the questions in an index go together, from 0 to 1. It rises with the number of questions, and it can be high even when the questions measure two different things, as here. When the errors in the answers are unrelated, alpha is a lower bound on the reliability of the index, equal to it only if every question measures the same thing equally well. When the errors are related, as a shared habit of answering makes them, alpha can overstate the reliability. Omega, under Advanced, uses the loadings of a factor model and gives the share of the index's variation that the factors account for; it is only as good as that model.

A factor model looks for a few unobserved variables, the factors, that would explain the correlations among the questions. A loading is the correlation between a question and a factor: near 0, the question has little to do with the factor; near 1, it follows the factor closely. The page fits the model as psych does by default, by making the correlations the model predicts as close as possible to the observed ones ("minres"). RMSEA, TLI, and BIC compare how well models with different numbers of factors fit. Parallel analysis keeps each factor whose eigenvalue, a measure of how much of the shared variation the factor takes, is larger than in 95% of random data sets of the same size. Fitted to random data, a one-factor model often lets a single question take nearly the whole factor, so the yardstick for the first factor is close to 1; psych does the same. Chapter 6 of Revelle's online book is a readable guide to all of this, with R code (Revelle, in preparation).

Rotation changes the axes, not the model: every rotation of the same solution fits the data equally well. Varimax keeps the axes at right angles and makes the loadings on each factor as unequal as possible, a few large and the rest near zero. Promax, oblimin, and geomin let the axes lean toward each other, so that the factors can correlate. Promax raises the varimax loadings to the fourth power to make a target and then finds the leaning axes that come closest to it. Oblimin, the default in psych, and geomin, the default of the efa() function in the R package lavaan, each reward loadings near zero in their own way. Factor scores give each respondent a value on each factor; for two or more factors the page uses psych's default, which keeps the scores correlated as the factors are.

A correlation between questions with three answers understates how strongly the opinions behind them go together. Polychoric correlations, under Advanced, estimate the latter by treating each answer as a cut through an opinion that varies smoothly. The loadings rise, but which question goes with which hardly changes.

The two factors follow the direction of the statements: the statements of harm go together, and so do the statements of benefit. Part of this split can come from a habit of agreeing with whatever statement is put to the respondent, which pushes answers to benefit and harm statements in opposite directions once the harm statements are reversed. Fitted to the eight statements alone, a two-factor model puts the correlation between the benefit and the harm factor at 0.44. Adding the habit as one more factor, acting equally on all eight statements, improves the fit and raises that correlation to 0.84 (Billiet and McClendon 2000). The code for that model is at the end of the R code. Because the benefit and the harm statements are worded in opposite directions, any difference between groups in that habit shows up as a difference between the two factors, so these questions cannot fully separate the two. Holding both views is also possible: a respondent can believe that immigrants fill useful jobs and that they raise unemployment. The regressions describe who holds which view; they do not say why.
