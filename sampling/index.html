Reading the picture

Each block in the plot is one sample’s estimate of the share of red marbles. The blocks gather around the bowl’s true share, and their spread is the sampling error of a single poll. The shaded band marks where theory expects 95 of every 100 estimates to fall, and its half-width is the margin of error.

Hold the sample at 100 and switch the bowl among 1,000, 10,000, and 100,000 marbles. The spread barely changes; between the smallest and the largest of these bowls, theory puts the difference at about 5 percent of the spread itself. Then hold the bowl fixed and quadruple the sample, from 25 to 100 and then to 400. Each quadrupling halves the spread, because the standard error falls with the square root of the sample size. Precision is bought with the absolute size of the sample, not with the fraction of the population it covers.

The size of the bowl enters only through the finite population correction, the factor √[(N − n)/(N − 1)] (Kish 1965). It departs appreciably from one only when the sample is a sizable share of the bowl. Draw all 1,000 marbles from a bowl of 1,000 and the spread vanishes, since a census has no sampling error. For an adult population numbering in the millions, the correction for a sample of 1,000 is one for every practical purpose, which is why such a sample yields a margin of roughly three points whether the country is Denmark or the United States.

The bowl is a best case. Every marble has the same chance of selection, and every marble reports its color faithfully. Surveys of people must also contend with coverage, nonresponse, and measurement error, none of which diminishes as the sample grows (Groves et al. 2009). The margin of error printed beneath a poll describes only the error this bowl can show.

How large a sample does a 95 percent interval require?

Let p be the share of red marbles in the bowl and p̂ the share in a simple random sample of n marbles. When the bowl is large relative to the sample, p̂ is centered on p, and by the central limit theorem its sampling distribution is approximately normal. About 95 percent of a normal distribution lies within 1.96 standard errors of its mean, so

SE(p̂) = √[p(1 − p)/n],   Pr(|p̂ − p| ≤ 1.96 SE) ≈ 0.95.

The inequality inside the parentheses can be read in two directions. As written, it says that 95 percent of samples land inside the band drawn in the plot above. Rearranged, it says that the interval p̂ ± E, where E = 1.96 SE, covers the true share in 95 percent of samples. The confidence attaches to the procedure and not to any single interval, which either covers p or does not.

To find the sample size, fix the margin E one is prepared to tolerate and solve for n:

E = 1.96 √[p(1 − p)/n]   ⇒   n0 = 1.962 p(1 − p)/E2.

The unknown share appears on the right-hand side, but p(1 − p) reaches its maximum of 1/4 at p = 1/2, so the conservative choice, which holds whatever the true share, is

n0 = 1.962/(4E2) = 3.8416/(4 × 0.032) ≈ 1067.1 for E = 0.03.

Rounding up, 1,068 observations guarantee a margin of no more than 3 points for any share. Because E enters squared, precision is expensive: halving the margin to 1.5 points quadruples the requirement to 4,269, and a margin of 1 point requires 9,604.

The size of the population has not yet appeared. It enters only because the bowl is sampled without replacement, which multiplies the variance by the finite population correction:

Var(p̂) = [p(1 − p)/n] · [(N − n)/(N − 1)].

Setting 1.962 Var(p̂) = E2 and solving for n gives (Cochran 1977)

n = n0N/(N − 1 + n0) = n0/[1 + (n0 − 1)/N].

As N grows, the denominator approaches one and n approaches n0 from below. For a margin of 3 points, a population of 10,000 requires 965 observations, one of 100,000 requires 1,056, and one of 250 million requires 1,068. However large the population, the requirement never exceeds n0. The calculator beside this text draws the curve for any margin.

Two cautions keep the result honest. First, the margin refers to a single share estimated from the whole sample. An estimate for a subgroup rests on fewer cases and carries a wider margin, and the lead of one candidate over another is estimated less precisely than either share, since

Var(p̂1 − p̂2) = [p1 + p2 − (p1 − p2)2]/n.

When two candidates divide the vote evenly this equals 1/n, so the standard error of the lead is twice that of either share. Second, the factor 1.96 rests on the normal approximation, which is poor when np or n(1 − p) is small; for samples of five marbles, the band in the plot is only a rough guide.
