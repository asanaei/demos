Learning the share without learning who

Some respondents will not admit that a woman serving as president would upset them. The item count technique and the crosswise model let each respondent keep that answer private while still revealing how common it is. Both depend on respondents following the instructions. Under "What can go wrong" you can break that assumption, and under "Safeguards" repair it.

Reading the picture

In the item count technique, a coin assigns each respondent to one of two lists that differ only by the sensitive item. Respondents report how many items apply, never which. Because assignment is random, the two groups agree on average about the shared items, so the difference between their average counts estimates the share for whom the sensitive item is true (Miller 1984; Glynn 2013). The sensitive item follows the list experiment of Streb et al. (2008).

In the crosswise model, everyone answers the same question about two statements: the sensitive one, and one whose prevalence p is known but which no researcher can observe for any individual, such as a mother's birth month. Respondents say only whether the two statements agree. If no one held the view, the share answering "both or neither" would be 1 - p; the shortfall from that benchmark identifies the prevalence (Yu, Tian, and Tang 2008; Jann, Jerke, and Krumpal 2012).

Both estimators are noisier than an honest direct question, and the crosswise model's noise grows without bound as p approaches one half, where an answer carries no information at all. Too little noise fails the other way: with a rare birth-month condition, "exactly one" points strongly to a holder, and in the item count technique anyone who reports every item on version B reveals the sensitive answer.

Beyond careless answering, the item count breaks in two places that "What can go wrong" opens. When every item applies, the count gives the respondent away, and some will report one fewer; the more upsetting the control items, the more respondents this reaches (Kuklinski, Cobb, and Gilens 1997; Glynn 2013). Glynn also names near-ceiling respondents who report one fewer, which the settings do not model, so they understate the problem. And the sensitive item can change how the others are counted, a design effect that the difference in means cannot tell apart from prevalence (Blair and Imai 2012).

The crosswise model breaks where the instructions do. A respondent who clicks at random says "both or neither" about half the time whatever the truth, which pulls every estimate toward one half: where no one holds the view, the false positives equal half the share who answer at random (Höglinger and Diekmann 2017). In two online surveys, Wolter and Diekmann (2021) found crosswise estimates of 5 to 15 percent for traits that almost no one has, while the item count technique mostly did not produce false positives. Here both techniques suffer equally from random answers; the item count's better record in their data is an empirical result this model does not generate. They trace the crosswise results to careless answering and to unrelated statements that were more or less common than assumed. With p below one half, a statement B that is more common than assumed is read as prevalence of the view.

The safeguards are repairs these papers point toward. Glynn (2013) shows that opposed control items lower the variance of the counts and keep every item from applying at once; the pairs here are perfectly opposed, a best case, and his own example cut the standard deviation of the baseline counts from 0.76 to 0.65. Wolter and Diekmann recommend building in a check for false positives; the placebo statement is such a check, and the adjustment that subtracts its estimate follows Schnapp (2019), whose correction for random answers they tested with respondents' own reports and could not recommend. The placebo here is as common as assumed, so it catches careless answering and leaves a flawed statement B alone; mother's and father's birthday statements in their data were both more common than expected (Table 4), and a placebo that shares B's flaw would catch that too. Replacing the assumed share with one measured in a randomly assigned direct-question group cut their false positives a good deal but not to zero. A screen removes some careless answers and some careful respondents with them: dropping everyone who sped through the questions would have removed 58 to 67 percent of their Swiss sample, and asking respondents whether they had answered carefully did not help, since almost none admitted to clicking at random. Apart from the opposed list, each repair costs precision, so compare the intervals with and without.

The settings are assumptions, not estimates. How many holders really hold back at the ceiling, or how far real birth months stray from the assumed shares, are questions for validation studies; the panel shows what each would cost. The direct question is not affected by any of them here. Glynn (2013) also describes the double list experiment, in which every respondent answers two lists and the two estimates are averaged; it is not simulated here.
